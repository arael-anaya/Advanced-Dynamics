\section{Introduction and Relevance}

The spring-loaded inverted pendulum (SLIP) is the reduced-order model
behind most legged-robot dynamics \cite{full1999,saranli2001}: a point
mass bouncing on a massless spring leg. Despite its simplicity, it
captures the center-of-mass dynamics of running in both animals and
machines remarkably well \cite{blickhan1989,mcmahon1990}. That makes it
a good subject for this course: it is simple enough to analyze fully
with the tools covered here, but rich enough to produce genuinely
interesting hybrid dynamics. It also builds directly on the elastic
pendulum studied in class, since the stance phase of SLIP is an
inverted spring pendulum.

What makes SLIP especially interesting from a mechanics standpoint is
that it is a hybrid dynamical system: during stance the body behaves
like a spring-loaded pendulum, while in flight it is a free projectile,
with the two phases connected by touchdown and liftoff switching
conditions. Rather than analyzing stability at a fixed equilibrium
point, the more natural question for a hopping gait is whether the
periodic motion itself is stable. The standard tool for that is the
apex return map, a Poincar\'e map in which a steady hopping gait
appears as a fixed point whose stability can be assessed numerically
\cite{blickhan1989,schwind2000}.

Beyond being a convenient classroom system, SLIP-type models are used
directly in the design and control of running robots and in the
biomechanical study of animal and human locomotion, which is the
practical motivation for this project: a model simple enough to
analyze by hand but predictive enough to inform how a real legged
system should be built or controlled.

\subsection*{Relation to Prior Work}
This project sits within an established line of SLIP research, and
each stage of the model draws on a different piece of that literature.
Blickhan \cite{blickhan1989} introduced the spring-mass template and
showed that it reproduces the ground-reaction-force and center-of-mass
patterns of human and animal running; the stance-phase Lagrangian
derived here reproduces that same reduced-order model, but from
Lagrange's equations in polar coordinates rather than Blickhan's
Newtonian force balance. McMahon and Cheng \cite{mcmahon1990} used the
same template to relate leg stiffness to running speed empirically;
the parameter sweep over stiffness $k$ and touchdown angle
$\theta_{TD}$ planned for this project asks the same question, but
numerically, from the model directly, rather than from experimental
data. Full and Koditschek's templates-and-anchors framework
\cite{full1999} is the justification for treating SLIP as a legitimate
object of study on its own, rather than only as a shortcut on the way
to a fully articulated, multi-link leg model: a template is expected
to capture the essential dynamics of the anchored system while
discarding detail that does not matter for stability. Schwind and
Koditschek \cite{schwind2000} derived an approximate, closed-form
stance map for SLIP using a perturbative solution; this project instead
integrates the stance-phase equations numerically with an event-based
solver, trading their analytical insight for an exact (if less
interpretable) stance map, consistent with the computational tools
emphasized in this course. Saranli, Buehler, and Koditschek
\cite{saranli2001} built RHex, a hexapod whose individual legs behave
like a SLIP template during stance, which is the practical grounding
for why this abstraction is worth studying: it is not just a toy
system, it is the model actually used to understand a working robot.
Finally, Geyer, Seyfarth, and Blickhan \cite{geyer2006} extended a
SLIP-type compliant-leg model to walking, not just running, by allowing
double support; that extension is a natural direction for future work
beyond the single-support hopping model considered here.

\section{Modeling of Dynamics}

The equations of motion for both phases of the SLIP model are derived
using \textbf{Lagrange's equations of motion}, with generalized
coordinates chosen separately for each phase: Cartesian coordinates
$(x,y)$ for the flight phase, and polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ about
the planted foot for the stance phase. The two phases are connected by
touchdown and liftoff switching (transversality) conditions, so the
overall model is a hybrid dynamical system integrated with event-based
numerical methods. Figure~\ref{fig:progress} shows the geometry of
both phases.

    $r,\theta$) and flight (projectile).}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Flight-Phase Equations of Motion}
With basis $\hat n_1,\hat n_2$, $\vec r_m=x\hat n_1+y\hat n_2$:
\[
    T=\frac12 m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2), \qquad V=mgy, \qquad
    L=T-V=\frac12m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)-mgy
\]
Lagrange's equations give
\begin{result}
\[
    \ddot x = 0, \qquad \ddot y = -g
\]
\end{result}
Starting at the apex ($\dot y=0$), with $x(0)=x_0$, $\dot x(0)=v_{x0}$:
\[
    x(t)=v_{x0}t+x_0, \qquad y(t)=y_0-\frac{g}{2}t^2
\]

\subsection{Touchdown: Detection and Transition Map}
$\theta_{TD}$ is held fixed in flight, so the foot trails the body at
\[
    \vec r_{foot}(t) = \big(x(t)-r_0\cos\theta_{TD}\big)\hat n_1
    + \big(y(t)-r_0\sin\theta_{TD}\big)\hat n_2
\]
Touchdown is $y_{foot}=0$, i.e. $y_{TD}=r_0\sin\theta_{TD}$. Solving
$y(t)=y_0-\tfrac g2t^2$:
\begin{result}
\[
    t_{TD}=\sqrt{\frac{2(y_0-r_0\sin\theta_{TD})}{g}}, \qquad
    \dot y_{TD}=-\sqrt{2g(y_0-r_0\sin\theta_{TD})}, \qquad
    x_{TD}=v_{x0}t_{TD}, \qquad \dot x_{TD}=v_{x0}
\]
\end{result}
Stance uses polar coordinates about the planted foot, so this Cartesian
state must convert. With foot at
$\vec r_F=(x_{TD}+r_0\cos\theta_{TD})\hat n_1$ and leg vector
$\vec\rho=\vec r_m-\vec r_F=-r_0\cos\theta_{TD}\,\hat n_1+y_{TD}\hat n_2$:
\[
    r = |\vec\rho| = \sqrt{r_0^2\cos^2\theta_{TD}+y_{TD}^2} = r_0, \qquad
    \theta = \operatorname{atan2}\!\big(y_{TD},\,-r_0\cos\theta_{TD}\big)
\]
Projecting $\vec v=\dot x_{TD}\hat n_1+\dot y_{TD}\hat n_2$ onto
$\hat e_r=\cos\theta\,\hat n_1+\sin\theta\,\hat n_2$,
$\hat e_\theta=-\sin\theta\,\hat n_1+\cos\theta\,\hat n_2$:
\begin{result}
\[
    \dot r = \vec v\cdot\hat e_r = \dot x_{TD}\cos\theta+\dot y_{TD}\sin\theta,
    \qquad
    \dot\theta = \frac{\vec v\cdot\hat e_\theta}{r_0}
    = \frac{-\dot x_{TD}\sin\theta+\dot y_{TD}\cos\theta}{r_0}
\]
\end{result}
implemented as \texttt{touchdownState()}.

\subsection{Stance-Phase Equations of Motion}
In stance the leg pivots about the fixed foot: $\vec r_m=r\hat e_r$,
$\vec v_m = \dot r\hat e_r+r\dot\theta\hat e_\theta$:
\[
    T=\frac12m\dot r^2+\frac12mr^2\dot\theta^2, \qquad
    V=mgr\sin\theta+\frac12k(r-r_0)^2, \qquad
    L=T-V
\]
Lagrange's equations:
\begin{result}
\[
    m\ddot r-mr\dot\theta^2+mg\sin\theta+k(r-r_0)=0, \qquad
    mr^2\ddot\theta+2mr\dot r\dot\theta+mgr\cos\theta=0
\]
\end{result}
solved for the highest derivatives:
\begin{result}
\[
    \ddot r = r\dot\theta^2-g\sin\theta-\frac{k}{m}(r-r_0), \qquad
    \ddot\theta = -\frac{2}{r}\dot r\dot\theta-\frac{g}{r}\cos\theta
\]
\end{result}
implemented as \texttt{stance\_RHS()}.

\subsection{Conserved Energy in Stance}
$\vec r_m=r\hat e_r$ is scleronomic and $T$ is homogeneous quadratic in
$(\dot r,\dot\theta)$, so $H=T+V$; with no explicit $t$-dependence,
energy is conserved through stance:
\[
    E = \frac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)+mgr\sin\theta
    +\frac12k(r-r_0)^2 = \text{const.}
\]
Neither coordinate is cyclic, so both equations must be integrated
together; this conserved quantity is used below as a check on the
numerical integrator.

\subsection{Liftoff Map and the Apex Return Map}
\label{sec:returnmap}
Liftoff occurs when the leg returns to its natural length with
positive rate, $r=r_0,\ \dot r>0$, at which point the stance-phase
state $(r_0,\theta_{LO},\dot r_{LO},\dot\theta_{LO})$ must be converted
back into Cartesian flight variables to begin the next flight phase.
Chaining flight $\to$ touchdown $\to$ stance $\to$ liftoff $\to$ flight
once defines a map from one apex state to the next,
$P:(y_{n},v_{x,n})\mapsto(y_{n+1},v_{x,n+1})$, whose fixed points
correspond to steady periodic hopping and whose local stability is
governed by the linearization of $P$ about that fixed point. Section
2.7 shows that this apparently two-dimensional map is in fact governed
by a single scalar, so that linearization turns out to be one
derivative, not a Jacobian's worth of eigenvalues.

Liftoff occurs at $r=r_0$ (the switching condition of
Section~2.3), so the liftoff state is
$(r_0,\theta_{LO},\dot r_{LO},\dot\theta_{LO})$. Position converts back
to Cartesian with the same relation used throughout stance,
$\vec r_m=r\hat e_r$ (Section~2.3), evaluated at $r=r_0$,
$\theta=\theta_{LO}$:
\[
    x_{LO}=r_0\cos\theta_{LO}, \qquad y_{LO}=r_0\sin\theta_{LO}
\]
Velocity converts by substituting $\hat e_r=\cos\theta\,\hat n_1+\sin\theta\,\hat n_2$,
$\hat e_\theta=-\sin\theta\,\hat n_1+\cos\theta\,\hat n_2$ (Section~2.2)
into $\vec v_m=\dot r\hat e_r+r\dot\theta\hat e_\theta$ (Section~2.3):
\[
    \vec v_m = \dot r\big(\cos\theta\,\hat n_1+\sin\theta\,\hat n_2\big)
    +r\dot\theta\big(-\sin\theta\,\hat n_1+\cos\theta\,\hat n_2\big)
    = \big(\dot r\cos\theta-r\dot\theta\sin\theta\big)\hat n_1
    +\big(\dot r\sin\theta+r\dot\theta\cos\theta\big)\hat n_2
\]
evaluated at liftoff ($r=r_0$, $\theta=\theta_{LO}$, $\dot r=\dot r_{LO}$,
$\dot\theta=\dot\theta_{LO}$):
\begin{result}
\[
    \dot x_{LO}=\dot r_{LO}\cos\theta_{LO}-r_0\dot\theta_{LO}\sin\theta_{LO},
    \qquad
    \dot y_{LO}=\dot r_{LO}\sin\theta_{LO}+r_0\dot\theta_{LO}\cos\theta_{LO}
\]
\end{result}
Note that this uses $\dot\theta_{LO}$, not just $\dot r_{LO}$: leg
rotation rate contributes to Cartesian velocity just as much as leg
extension rate does, through the $r\dot\theta\hat e_\theta$ term of
$\vec v_m$.

Once airborne, $r$ and $\theta$ have no physical meaning to condition
on: they are polar coordinates about the planted foot, and there is no
foot left to be ``about'' after liftoff releases it. Apex is instead
defined purely in the ground frame, by $\dot y=0$. Flight has
$\ddot y=-g$ (Section~2.1), so, starting the clock at liftoff,
\[
    \dot y(t) = \dot y_{LO}-gt
\]
Setting $\dot y(t_{apex})=0$:
\begin{result}
\[
    t_{apex} = \frac{\dot y_{LO}}{g}
\]
\end{result}
This is linear in $t$ because it is a condition on velocity, one
integration of the constant $-g$ away from $t=0$. Touchdown
(Section~2.2) instead solves a condition on position,
$y(t_{TD})=y_{TD}$, and $y(t)=y_0-\tfrac g2t^2$ is quadratic in $t$
(two integrations of $-g$) -- which is why that closed form needed a
square root and this one does not.

Propagating $x,y,\dot x,\dot y$ forward from liftoff using the flight
solution of Section~2.1, $x(t)=\dot x_{LO}t+x_{LO}$,
$y(t)=y_{LO}+\dot y_{LO}t-\tfrac g2t^2$, $\dot x(t)=\dot x_{LO}$,
$\dot y(t)=\dot y_{LO}-gt$, at $t=t_{apex}=\dot y_{LO}/g$:
\begin{result}
\[
    x_{n+1} = x_{LO}+\dot x_{LO}\,t_{apex}, \qquad
    y_{n+1} = y_{LO}+\dot y_{LO}\,t_{apex}-\frac g2t_{apex}^2, \qquad
    v_{x,n+1}=\dot x_{LO}, \qquad
    \dot y_{n+1}=0
\]
\end{result}
$v_{x,n+1}=\dot x_{LO}$ exactly, since horizontal motion is unforced in
flight ($\ddot x=0$, Section~2.1); $\dot y_{n+1}=0$ exactly, by
construction of $t_{apex}$, not approximately.

The returned apex state $(x_{n+1},y_{n+1},v_{x,n+1},\dot y_{n+1})$ is
expressed in Cartesian coordinates, not polar. This is a deliberate
representational choice, not an oversight: polar $(r,\theta)$ was only
ever meaningful about the specific planted foot of the stance phase
just finished, and once airborne there is no foot location left for
the next flight segment to be polar about. ($x_{LO}$ above is likewise
expressed relative to that same foot rather than to some fixed ground
origin; Section~2.7 shows this choice of origin cannot matter, since
$x$ never enters the dynamics of either phase.)

\subsection{Conserved Energy at Apex and Across a Full Hop Cycle}
The flight-phase Lagrangian (Section~2.1) has $\vec r_m=x\hat n_1+y\hat
n_2$ scleronomic and $T=\tfrac12m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)$ homogeneous
quadratic in $(\dot x,\dot y)$, with no explicit $t$-dependence -- the
same structural conditions used in Section~2.4 for stance. By the
identical argument, $H=T+V$ is conserved through flight:
\[
    E = \frac12m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)+mgy = \text{const.}
\]
with no spring term, since the leg carries no elastic potential energy
while airborne (unloaded, uncompressed). This is the apex-state energy
functional. It differs from the stance-phase functional of
Section~2.4,
\[
    E_{stance}=\frac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)+mgr\sin\theta
    +\frac12k(r-r_0)^2,
\]
by the missing $\frac12k(r-r_0)^2$ term. The two functionals coincide
exactly at touchdown and at liftoff, because both switching events
occur at $r=r_0$ (Sections~2.2, 2.3), where the spring term vanishes
identically, $\frac12k(r_0-r_0)^2=0$. Total mechanical energy is
therefore continuous across every touchdown and liftoff transition,
and, since it is separately conserved within each phase, it is
conserved across an entire cycle apex $\to$ touchdown $\to$ stance
$\to$ liftoff $\to$ apex. Section~4.2 validates this numerically.

\subsection{Dimension Reduction of the Apex State}
At apex, $\dot y=0$ by definition (Section~2.5). Substituting into the
apex energy functional of Section~2.6:
\[
    E = \frac12m\dot x^2+mgy
\]
Solving for $\dot x$:
\[
    E-mgy = \frac12m\dot x^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \dot x^2 = \frac{2(E-mgy)}{m} = \frac{2E}{m}-2gy
\]
\begin{result}
\[
    \dot x = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m}-2gy} \qquad (\dot x>0\text{ for forward hopping})
\]
\end{result}
The apex state $(x,y,\dot x,\dot y)$ is therefore not four independent
numbers. $\dot y=0$ identically at every apex, by definition. $x$
never appears in any potential or force in either phase
(Sections~2.1, 2.3): it is an ignorable coordinate, free to translate
rigidly from hop to hop without feeding back into $\dot x$, $y$, or
$\dot y$ -- which is exactly why Section~2.5 was free to express
$x_{LO}$ relative to the current stance foot rather than to a fixed
ground origin. And $\dot x$ is not independently free: once $E$ is
fixed, it is determined by $y$ through the relation above. So at fixed
$E$, the entire apex state collapses to the single scalar $y$, and the
hop-to-hop dynamics reduce to a one-dimensional map,
\[
    y_{n+1} = P(y_n)
\]
where $P$ is the composition: reconstruct the full apex state from
$y_n$ via $\dot x_n=\sqrt{2E/m-2gy_n}$ (this section) $\to$ flight to
touchdown (Section~2.2) $\to$ stance integration to liftoff
(Section~2.3) $\to$ flight to the next apex (Section~2.5).

This reduction was checked numerically, not just asserted
algebraically: the closed-form $\dot x$ above, evaluated at the
\textit{output} apex height $y_{n+1}$ produced by simulating one full
hop, was compared against the $\dot x_{n+1}$ that hop actually
produced. Section~4.2 reports the result: the two agree to
machine precision.

\subsection{The Apex Return Map: Fixed Point and Stability Criterion}
\label{sec:apexmapdef}
With the reduction of Section~2.7, define the apex return map $P$ at
fixed leg stiffness $k$ and fixed touchdown angle $\theta_{TD}$, with
total energy $E$ also held fixed:
\[
    P: y_n \mapsto y_{n+1}
\]
$E$, $k$, and $\theta_{TD}$ play different roles. $E$ characterizes
how fast, or how energetic, a given running gait is -- a property of
the apex state a gait starts from (Section~2.6), not something swept.
$k$ and $\theta_{TD}$ are the only two free parameters of the model
itself (Section~2.3): there is no controller anywhere in this system,
so a periodic gait at a given $E$ is whatever $P$ happens to produce
for a chosen $(k,\theta_{TD})$, not something steered toward stability.

A periodic (self-repeating) gait is a fixed point of $P$: a height
$y^\ast$ such that
\[
    P(y^\ast) = y^\ast
\]
equivalently, a root of
\[
    f(y) = P(y)-y
\]
Local stability of $y^\ast$ is governed by the derivative of $P$ at
the fixed point, $P'(y^\ast)$, estimated by a central finite
difference:
\[
    P'(y^\ast) \approx \frac{P(y^\ast+dy)-P(y^\ast-dy)}{2\,dy}
\]
\[
    |P'(y^\ast)|<1: \text{ stable (perturbations shrink hop over hop)}
\]
\[
    |P'(y^\ast)|>1: \text{ unstable (perturbations grow)}
\]
The sign of $P'(y^\ast)$ carries meaning beyond the magnitude:
$P'(y^\ast)<0$ means a perturbation's sign flips every hop --
oscillatory divergence or convergence, alternating above and below
$y^\ast$ -- while $P'(y^\ast)>0$ means a perturbation's sign is
preserved every hop -- monotonic divergence or convergence, drifting
away from or toward $y^\ast$ on one side. Section~4.3 reports the
root-finding methodology and the result for the validated parameter
set of Section~4.1.

\section{Discussion of Assumptions}

Several modeling choices were made to keep the SLIP model tractable
with the tools of this course, each with a corresponding limitation:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
    \item \textbf{Planar motion.} The model is restricted to the
    sagittal plane. Real running involves some lateral and rotational
    motion of the trunk and legs, which this model cannot capture.
    \item \textbf{Point mass, massless leg.} All body mass is lumped
    at a single point, and the leg itself carries no mass or rotational
    inertia. This isolates the center-of-mass dynamics that SLIP is
    meant to describe, but discards any effect of leg-swing inertia or
    trunk pitch, which the templates-and-anchors view
    \cite{full1999} explicitly treats as detail that a template is
    allowed to omit.
    \item \textbf{Linear (Hookean) leg spring.} The stance leg is
    modeled as a single ideal linear spring, $F=-k(r-r_0)$, with
    constant stiffness $k$. Biological and robotic legs have leg
    stiffness that is nonlinear and can be actively modulated
    by the neuromuscular system or a controller; treating $k$ as a
    fixed parameter to be swept is a simplification, not a claim that
    real legs behave this way.
    \item \textbf{Fixed touchdown angle held through flight.} $\theta_{TD}$
    is assumed constant for the entire flight phase, i.e. there is no
    swing-leg retraction or angle adjustment in response to a
    disturbance before the foot lands. This is the standard SLIP
    assumption, but it means the model's only ``control input'' between
    hops is the touchdown angle chosen before each flight, not a
    continuous correction during flight.
    \item \textbf{Instantaneous, rigid, non-slipping foot placement.}
    Touchdown and liftoff are treated as instantaneous events with no
    impact losses, and the foot is assumed to stick to the ground
    (no slipping, no ground compliance) for the duration of stance at
    a fixed height $y=0$. Real contact involves finite compliance and
    a friction cone that could, in principle, be violated at high
    touchdown angles or high speeds.
    \item \textbf{No stance-phase dissipation, no aerodynamic drag.}
    The stance-phase Lagrangian has no damping term, so mechanical
    energy is exactly conserved within a single stance phase (verified
    numerically in Section~\ref{sec:results}); flight is likewise
    treated as drag-free projectile motion. This was a deliberate
    choice: it isolates the elastic dynamics of interest and gives a
    clean, near-machine-precision check on the numerical integrator,
    at the cost of neglecting the fact that any real leg or running
    surface dissipates some energy each stride.
    \item \textbf{Equal rest length at touchdown and liftoff.} The leg
    is assumed to return to the same natural length $r_0$ between hops,
    with no active leg extension/retraction actuation changing that
    rest length from one stance phase to the next.
\end{itemize}

\end{insertBox}

\subsection{Full-Cycle Energy Conservation and Dimension-Reduction Validation}
Beyond the single-stance check of Section~4.1,
\texttt{proveX\_dotIsRedundant()} validates the full hop cycle. Starting from the apex
state of Section~4.1 ($y_0=1.2$ m, $v_{x0}=3.0$ m/s), it computes the
apex energy $E_1$ from the functional of Section~2.6
(\texttt{energyHop()}), chains one entire hop -- flight $\to$
touchdown $\to$ stance $\to$ liftoff $\to$ flight (\texttt{hop()}) --
to the next apex, and recomputes the apex energy $E_2$ there. Relative
drift over the full cycle is
\[
    \frac{|E_2-E_1|}{|E_1|} = 6.17\times10^{-13}
\]
consistent with the stance-phase-only drift of Section~4.1
($6.46\times10^{-13}$), and confirming that total mechanical energy --
not just the stance-phase quantity of Section~2.4 -- is conserved
across the full switching cycle.

The same run cross-checks the dimension reduction of Section~2.7. With
$E_1$ fixed, the closed-form $\dot x=\sqrt{2E_1/m-2gy}$ is evaluated
at the simulated output apex height, $y_{n+1}=1.4465$ m, predicting
$\dot x_{pred}=2.0403$ m/s; the simulation's own output horizontal
velocity at that apex is $v_{x,n+1}=2.0403$ m/s. Relative difference:
\[
    \frac{|v_{x,n+1}-\dot x_{pred}|}{|\dot x_{pred}|} = 2.41\times10^{-12}
\]
i.e.\ the algebraic reduction of Section~2.7 is not only formally
correct, it is numerically consistent with the simulator to machine
precision.

\subsection{Apex Return Map: Root-Finding Methodology and Result}
\label{sec:apexmapresult}
\texttt{findApexMapFixedPoint()} locates the root of $f(y)=P(y)-y$
(Section~2.8) over the domain of $y$ that is physically valid for a
given $(k,\theta_{TD})$ and $E$. The domain is bounded below by
\[
    y_{min} = r_0\sin\theta_{TD}
\]
the minimum apex height geometrically consistent with a touchdown at
angle $\theta_{TD}$ (Section~2.2: the foot cannot be below ground at
touchdown, so $y_{TD}=r_0\sin\theta_{TD}\le y_0$). It is bounded above
by the height at which the closed-form $\dot x$ of Section~2.7 would
require a negative value under the square root,
\[
    \frac{2E}{m}-2gy\ge0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y\le y_{max}=\frac{E}{mg}
\]
At the exact lower boundary $y=y_{min}$, floating-point roundoff in
the touchdown free-fall relation
$\dot y_{TD}=-\sqrt{2g(y_0-r_0\sin\theta_{TD})}$ (Section~2.2) can push
the argument of that square root slightly negative even though it is
analytically exactly zero; a small inward padding,
$y_{min}\to y_{min}+\epsilon$ ($\epsilon=10^{-3}$ m in the
implementation), avoids this without altering the physics.

$f(y)$ is sampled coarsely across $[y_{min},y_{max}]$ (30 samples in
the implementation), and consecutive samples are checked for a sign
change. Once a bracket $[y_i,y_{i+1}]$ with
$\operatorname{sign}(f(y_i))\ne\operatorname{sign}(f(y_{i+1}))$ is
found, Brent's method refines it to a root $y^\ast$. The derivative
$P'(y^\ast)$ is then estimated by the central difference of
Section~2.8 with $dy=10^{-3}$ m.

For the validated parameter set of Section~4.1 ($m=80$ kg, $r_0=1$ m,
$k=20000$ N/m, $\theta_{TD}=68^\circ$, with $E$ fixed by the apex
state $y_0=1.2$ m, $v_{x0}=3.0$ m/s):
\begin{result}
\[
    y^\ast \approx 1.5096\text{ m}, \qquad P'(y^\ast) \approx -2.76
\]
\end{result}
$|P'(y^\ast)|>1$: this fixed point is unstable. The negative sign
further indicates oscillatory divergence -- a perturbed apex height
does not drift monotonically away from $y^\ast$, it overshoots on
alternating sides of $y^\ast$, growing in magnitude each hop. This is
a genuine finding of the analysis, not a discrepancy to be explained
away: $\theta_{TD}=68^\circ$ was the value the project's working
assumption had treated as the symmetric touchdown/liftoff angle, with
an informal expectation that such a symmetric sweep would correspond
to a stable periodic gait. It does not: at this $(k,\theta_{TD})$,
steady hopping at $y^\ast\approx1.5096$ m is unstable.

\subsection{Grid Sweep Methodology and Parameter Sweep}
\texttt{gridSearchFixedPoints()} wraps the single-case pipeline of
Section~4.3 into a function of $(k,\theta_{TD})$ that returns
$(y^\ast,P'(y^\ast))$, or a null result if
\texttt{findApexMapFixedPoint()} finds no sign change in $f(y)$ across
the valid domain for that pair -- i.e.\ no periodic gait exists there
at this $E$. Each grid cell is evaluated against its own deep copy of
the base parameter dictionary, with only $k$ overwritten per cell, so
sweeping $k$ across the grid never mutates the shared dictionary read
by other cells or other stages of the pipeline.

The grid itself is, for now, a coarse $5\times5$ sweep: $k$ from
$5000$ to $40000$ N/m (soft to stiff) and $\theta_{TD}$ from
$50^\circ$ to $80^\circ$, with each cell classified as stable
($|P'(y^\ast)|<1$), unstable ($|P'(y^\ast)|>1$), or
no-fixed-point-found. This coarse pass is intended to be refined near
the stability boundary and visualized as a contour or pcolormesh
figure over $(k,\theta_{TD})$, per the deliverable below. The
physically motivated bounds under consideration for that refinement
are a lower angle bound near $\theta_{TD}\approx59^\circ$ -- the
friction-cone floor found separately (constraint-force analysis at
$\mu=0.6$), below which the required ground-reaction force cannot be
sustained without the foot slipping -- up through steep angles of
attack, and a $k$ range wide enough to span qualitatively soft to
stiff leg behavior.
